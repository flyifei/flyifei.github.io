Radio telephony and English

Twenty minutes a day, four drills in rotation.

  • Shadowing. Repeat ATC audio half a sentence behind the speaker, to work on rhythm and linking.
  • ATC dictation. Two minutes of tower frequency, written down: callsigns, instructions, runway. Then check.
  • Phraseology. Read Doc 9432 aloud until the standard calls come out without thought.
  • Ninety-second self-recording. Explain one flight in English, listen back, mark where you stalled.

Aviation English

Selection includes an ICAO English test, and the licence requires ICAO Language Proficiency level 4 or above. This is my working set: what gets assessed, the standard calls I drill, and the words I keep having to look up.

What is assessed

AreaWhat the examiner is listening forWhat I do about it
PronunciationClear, consistent delivery that a controller can decode first time, dialect notwithstanding.Record 90 seconds, play back, mark the words I slur.
StructureBasic sentence forms used correctly; errors are tolerated only when meaning survives.Drill one structure per week: “request…”, “unable…”, “confirm…”.
VocabularyEnough plain and aviation-specific words to handle routine, unusual and emergency situations.Keep the word lists below and add to them from real transmissions.
FluencyTalking at a natural pace without long, meaning-destroying pauses.Speak without stopping for 2 minutes on one topic, daily.
ComprehensionUnderstanding accented, fast or distorted speech in operational conditions.Daily ATC dictation from live feeds, then check against the audio.
InteractionsKeeping the exchange going: checking, clarifying, asking for a repeat.Practise “say again”, “confirm”, “unable” until they are automatic.

Standard calls I practise

Phraseology is defined in ICAO Doc 9432; local procedures may differ, so check the AIP as well. These are the ones that come up every session.

CallMeansWatch out for
Say againRepeat your last transmission.Add the reason only if it helps: “say again, blocked”.
StandbyWait — I will call you.It is not an approval of anything.
RogerI have received your last transmission.Never a clearance. Does not mean “yes”.
WilcoI understand and will comply.Only for instructions you can actually carry out.
Affirm / NegativeYes / no.Not “affirmative” in modern standard phraseology.
CorrectionI made an error; the correct version follows.Say it immediately — do not let a wrong readback stand.
Line up and waitEnter the runway and hold position.Replaced the old “position and hold”.
Cleared for take-offPermission to depart.No other call authorises departure.
Cleared to landPermission to land.Separate from “cleared for the approach”.
Hold short of runway 07LStop before the holding point.Read back the runway designator.
Climb / descend and maintain 5,000 ftReach and stay at that level.Read back the level, not just “roger”.
Report establishedAdvise when on the localiser / approach path.Say “established” only when both are true.
UnableI cannot comply.Add a reason if you can: “unable, performance”.
Go aroundAbandon the approach.Say it early; fly first, explain later.
Mayday ×3 / Pan-pan ×3Distress (grave and imminent danger) / urgency.Then callsign, nature, intentions, position, level, persons.

Word lists

Weather
  • CAVOK — ceiling and visibility OK: 10 km or more, no cloud below 5,000 ft, no significant weather.
  • SHRA — rain showers; TSRA — thunderstorm with rain.
  • FZFG — freezing fog; MIFG — shallow fog; BR — mist.
  • Wind shear — sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance.
  • Microburst — a concentrated downdraft; the one that ruins the approach.
  • QNH / QFE / QNE — sea-level pressure / field pressure / standard (1013.25 hPa).
Aircraft and systems
  • APU — auxiliary power unit; GPU — ground power unit.
  • Flaps / slats — high-lift devices on the trailing and leading edge.
  • Trim — the setting that removes steady control force.
  • TOGA — take-off / go-around thrust.
  • V1 / VR / V2 — decision speed, rotation speed, take-off safety speed.
  • MCDU / FMS — the box and the flight management system behind it.
ATC instructions
  • Cleared for the approach — permission to fly the published approach, not to land.
  • Expedite — do it promptly; “without delay” is stronger.
  • Cross at or above 3,000 ft — level restriction at a point, not a clearance to descend.
  • Squawk 7043 — set that code in the transponder.
  • Ident — press the ident button so the return flashes.
  • Number one for the ILS — your sequence in the arrival.
Emergencies
  • MAYDAY — distress: grave and imminent danger, need immediate assistance.
  • PAN-PAN — urgency: a situation that is concerning but not immediately life-threatening.
  • Declaring an emergency — say the words; the controller only needs the type and the intentions.
  • Negative contact / lost communications — say what you can and cannot hear.
  • Assistance required — equipment on the ground for the arrival.

Twenty minutes a day

  • 10 minutes shadowing. Repeat live ATC audio half a sentence behind the controller — rhythm and linking, not comprehension.
  • 5 minutes dictation. Write down callsigns, instructions and runway numbers, then check them against the recording.
  • 5 minutes speaking. Read one METAR aloud, decode it, and explain the flight you would plan. Record it; listen back once.

Self-check

  • Can I read a METAR aloud and explain every group without hesitating?
  • Can I give a position report: callsign, position, level, next point, time?
  • Can I say “unable” and offer an alternative, in one breath?
  • Can I follow a transmission with two instructions and read both back?
  • Can I keep talking for 90 seconds about a flight I actually made?

Phraseology and proficiency standards are set by ICAO; local procedures vary by state and by aerodrome. Nothing here replaces the AIP or the company manual.
